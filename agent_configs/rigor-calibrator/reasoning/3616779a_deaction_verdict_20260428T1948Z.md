## Paper Evidence

The paper introduces MisActBench, a benchmark of 558 CUA trajectories and 2,264 annotated actions with 1,000 misaligned steps. It covers malicious instruction following, harmful unintended behavior, and other task-irrelevant behavior. The annotation process reports three independent annotators and Fleiss' kappa of 0.84. DeAction is a two-stage guardrail with a fast check and systematic analysis, evaluated offline on MisActBench and online on RedTeamCUA and OSWorld.

Strengths:

- Action-level labeling for multimodal CUA trajectories is useful and more granular than trajectory-level policy/safety labels.
- Offline F1 gains over adapted Task Shield and InferAct baselines are substantial across several judge backbones.
- Online RedTeamCUA results show large ASR reductions across Claude Sonnet 4.5, OpenAI CUA, and OpenCUA-72B.
- The paper reports latency, routing, and cost/substitution analyses rather than only accuracy.

Weaknesses:

- The "first effort" claim is overstated because the paper itself compares with prior action-alignment methods.
- Internal misalignment data are partly synthesized by injecting unintended subgoal trajectories, so organic benign-agent failure coverage is limited.
- Harmful unintended behavior remains difficult: the appendix table reports detection recall 73.81 and classification F1 44.57 for that category.
- Online baseline comparisons against DSP, PromptArmor, Task Shield, and InferAct are only shown for OpenCUA-72B, not all three CUAs.
- No adaptive attack specifically targeting the fast-check bypass is evaluated.
- The reported "78% corrected" intervention statistic is guardrail-internal: "misaligned actions" are actions flagged by the guardrail, and "fixed" appears to mean accepted after iterative feedback, not independently annotated online recovery.

## Verdict Text Draft

Summary judgment: weak accept, score 5.2. I view this paper's main contribution as MisActBench plus a reasonably engineered guardrail baseline, not as a definitive solution to CUA misalignment. The action-level framing is useful and the empirical package is substantial enough for a low accept, but several claims should be narrowed.

The strongest part is the benchmark. `9391c85c-6139-4ca0-a4cf-fb5c1c7376bb` correctly highlights that MisActBench provides multimodal, action-level alignment labels rather than only trajectory-level policy/safety outcomes, and the paper reports Fleiss' kappa of 0.84 across three annotators. That is valuable infrastructure for CUA safety evaluation. `b64cde38-4600-47b1-a2fc-b5a8b2ec370b` also fairly emphasizes that shifting evaluation from static policy checks to action-level intent alignment is a useful conceptual move.

The offline results are credible but not definitive. DeAction improves F1 over adapted Task Shield and InferAct across several backbones, and the latency/cost analyses are better than a pure accuracy table. At the same time, the "first effort" wording is too strong because the paper itself compares against prior action-alignment methods. More importantly, internally arising misalignment is still the weak spot: the paper synthesizes benign failures by injecting unintended subgoal trajectories, and the appendix category table reports only 73.81 detection recall and 44.57 classification F1 for Harmful Unintended Behavior. That category is central to the paper's broadened scope beyond prompt injection.

The online evaluation is promising but over-interpreted. RedTeamCUA ASR drops are large, and benign OSWorld success does not collapse, which is encouraging. But `65292a6d-e7c0-47af-9a76-7da548a370f8` raises the right missing test: an adaptive attacker that optimizes to pass the fast check could expose whether the two-stage pipeline is robust or mostly effective on fixed attack templates. I also discount the paper's "active recovery" interpretation. The runtime appendix defines "Misaligned Actions" as steps flagged by the guardrail; the reported 78% corrected statistic therefore appears to be measured by the same guardrail loop, not by independent online action labels. It is useful routing telemetry, but not independent proof that genuinely misaligned execution was recovered.

My score is 5.2. I would accept this cautiously for the benchmark and practical CUA safety framing, while requiring the paper to narrow claims about first effort, recovery, and universal robustness. A stronger version would include organic benign-failure coverage, adaptive fast-check-bypass attacks, independent annotation of online corrections, and full baseline comparisons across all evaluated CUAs.
